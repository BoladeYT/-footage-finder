## PREMISE CARD: Amazon Alone
Status: Approved by the creator on 2026-10-31 (title kept as a leaning)

**Working promise:** Could you survive being lost alone in the Amazon, when the things that hurt you most aren't the ones you came in fearing?

**The premise:** "You" are lost and alone in the Amazon rainforest for several days, cut off from help. "You" expect the dangers from films, such as jaguars and snakes. The pressure may instead come from ordinary things: never getting dry, a small wound in heat and humidity, water "you" can't trust, and a canopy that hides the sun (all to check). The stakes: whether a run of small problems adds up to something life-threatening before "you" get out.

**Why this version:** Most jungle survival videos sell the big predators; this one is about the overlooked, ordinary things that may really decide whether "you" make it (to check).

**What's worth understanding (unverified):** How the rainforest actually wears a person down. Possibly that constant wetness, minor injuries, unsafe water and disorientation matter more over a few days than large animals. Possibly also how being lost in unfamiliar terrain affects judgment, and why a common movie belief about jungle danger misleads people.

**Likely engine (unverified):** Possibly the body as timeline: small problems compounding over the days. One option is a small wound acting as the story's clock. Being lost is a likely second pressure.

**Questions for Research (to check)**
1. For a lone person lost in lowland Amazon rainforest for a few days, what realistically threatens them most? Compare large predators and venomous animals against unsafe water and dehydration, wound infection, skin and foot damage from constant wetness, insects, and being cold and wet at night. How rare are jaguar attacks on people, and which animals (for example, venomous snakes) pose a realistic risk, and how?
2. How quickly can a minor cut or scrape become a serious problem in hot, humid conditions? What are the warning signs, and what does public first-aid guidance say about caring for it without supplies? Please assign safety levels.
3. Water: is collected rainwater safer than stream or river water? What treatment options do public-health bodies describe when "you" have little or no gear?
4. Why do people get disoriented under a rainforest canopy, and how close to a trail or camp do people realistically get lost? What does search-and-rescue guidance say about staying put versus moving, for example following water to a river?
5. How do region and season (wet or dry) change rainfall, night temperatures, flooding and the threats above? Which setting would give the story the most honest pressure?
6. Which popular jungle-survival beliefs are wrong or risky (for example, about drinking from vines, eating wild plants, or animal danger), and what is the correct picture?
