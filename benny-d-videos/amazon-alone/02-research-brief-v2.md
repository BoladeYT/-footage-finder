## ANSWERS TO THE PREMISE CARD'S QUESTIONS
1. What realistically threatens a lone person; how rare are jaguar attacks; which animals pose a realistic risk? → Jaguar attacks on people are rare: about 84 recorded cases in the Brazilian Amazon from 1950 to 2025, about 1.12 a year (F01, preprint). Venomous snakes are the realistic animal risk. *Bothrops* pit vipers cause about 80–90% of reported bites in the region, mostly on the lower limbs (F03, F04), with serious local tissue damage (F05). Among the ordinary dangers, verified mechanisms exist for: water that can't be judged by sight (F14); warm-water immersion foot after 48 hours or more of wet feet (F11); disorientation without the sun (F07, F08); and infection entering through broken skin (F12, F13, F19). Nights stay warm, with monthly minimums of about 23–24 °C at Manaus (F21). Hypothermia risk when wet at night is unresolved (X04).
2. How quickly a minor cut becomes a problem; warning signs; first-aid guidance without supplies → Warning signs and public cut care are verified (F12, F13). The speed of worsening in heat and humidity is unresolved (X01, X02). Guidance assumes clean water and dressings, which "you" won't have (F12, conditions).
3. Rainwater versus stream water; treatment with little gear → Water quality can't be judged by sight, smell or taste, and surface water in austere settings is likely poor (F14). Boiling reliably kills pathogens (F15). Solar disinfection works in full sun over 4–6 hours (F16), but the canopy blocks most sunlight (F08). Roof rainwater is generally safe when the collecting surface is clean; contamination comes mainly from the catchment (F17). No source addresses rain caught in a forest; that remains an extrapolation (F17, conditions).
4. Disorientation under canopy; how close people get lost; stay put or move → Without the sun or a distant landmark, people walk in circles without noticing (F07). The rainforest canopy blocks most direct light (F08). North American search-and-rescue guidance says to stop and usually stay put, signal in threes, and, if you must move, follow water (F10). How close to camp people get lost is unresolved (X06). Amazon-specific guidance was not found (X07).
5. How region and season change things → Central Amazon (Manaus normals): about 300–383 mm of rain a month from January to May, and about 106–128 mm in August and September; minimums 23–24 °C year-round (F21). Under the canopy it is cooler and more humid than in clearings, and much rain is intercepted by the canopy (F08, F18). Flood-season river levels were not researched (X12).
6. Popular jungle-survival beliefs that are wrong → Verified: the belief that big cats are the main danger is out of proportion (F01, F02). The belief that you walk straight when lost is wrong: people circle while convinced they're going straight (F07). Not researched: vine water, wild plants, piranhas (X08).

## PART A: FACT LEDGER

F01: Jaguar attacks on people in the Brazilian Amazon are rare: 84 recorded cases from 1950 to 2025, about 1.12 a year. That is far fewer than attacks by pumas, lions, tigers or leopards, though more than previously recognised.
- Type: Verified principle
- Evidence: S1 (preprint, not peer reviewed; measured in: compiled records of attacks in the Brazilian Amazon from field documentation, local news and scientific literature)
- Conditions and limits: Applies to the Brazilian Amazon. Recorded cases only; under-recording is possible. The authors' point is that attacks are more common than assumed, so "rare" should not become "never".
- Confidence: Medium (preprint; abstract only)
- Safety level: Not applicable
- Story relevance: The danger "you" fear most is real but statistically small.

F02: Most recorded jaguar-attack victims were adult men in rural areas (71 of 84). 52% were unaccompanied, and most were doing extractive or subsistence work. About half of the attacks were apparently unprovoked. Fatalities were more frequent when victims were alone or lacked defensive tools.
- Type: Verified principle
- Evidence: S1 (preprint; as F01)
- Conditions and limits: The victims were mostly forest-dependent residents working in the forest, not visitors. Being alone was associated with worse outcomes among people who were attacked. It does not establish how likely an attack is for a lone visitor.
- Confidence: Medium
- Safety level: Not applicable
- Story relevance: Gives an honest scale for any jaguar presence in the story.

F03: *Bothrops* pit vipers cause about 80–90% of reported snakebites in Brazil and the rest of the Amazon region.
- Type: Verified principle
- Evidence: S4 (peer-reviewed; the figure is stated in the paper's introduction, citing earlier literature)
- Conditions and limits: Reported bites only. The paper names the genus, not a single species, in the passage read.
- Confidence: Medium-High
- Safety level: Not applicable
- Story relevance: If an animal genuinely threatens "you", this is the realistic one.

F04: In a hospital series in Manaus (2012–2016, 307 complete records of *Bothrops* bites), 88% of bites were on the lower limb, 79% of patients were male, and 72% of bites happened in rural areas.
- Type: Verified principle
- Evidence: S4 (peer-reviewed; measured in: patients treated at one tertiary hospital in Manaus)
- Conditions and limits: Only people who reached a hospital, at one centre. Not a measure of how often bites happen.
- Confidence: Medium
- Safety level: Not applicable
- Story relevance: Legs and feet are where bites land, which ties to footwear and to where "you" step.

F05: *Bothrops* bites cause local pain, swelling, redness and bruising. Serious complications include blistering, abscess, tissue death (necrosis) and compartment syndrome. In the Manaus series, 19% of hospital-treated patients had severe tissue complications. WHO states that venomous bites can cause tissue destruction leading to permanent disability or amputation, as well as bleeding disorders and kidney failure.
- Type: Verified principle
- Evidence: S4 (peer-reviewed; hospital patients, mostly treated with antivenom); S2 (official WHO fact sheet; global)
- Conditions and limits: The 19% figure is for treated patients. Outcomes without treatment are not quantified here. No timeline is given for how fast the effects develop.
- Confidence: High (WHO plus peer-reviewed)
- Safety level: Open (recognising the effects)
- Story relevance: An honest-sized real danger. It is serious, but it is not a jaguar.

F06: WHO first aid for snakebite (Box 1): transport the person to a health facility as soon as possible; remove anything tight from the bitten part (rings, anklets, bracelets) in case of swelling; reassure the victim, since many bites are from non-venomous snakes, and even after most venomous bites the risk of death is not immediate; immobilise the person completely, splint the limb and carry them on a makeshift stretcher; never use a tight arterial tourniquet; avoid traditional methods such as cutting, suction or "black stones". The pressure-immobilisation bandage is recommended only for neurotoxic snakes that don't cause local swelling. Many victims die in transit from lying on their backs; keep them on their left side, mouth turned down. Long walks to care delay treatment and accelerate venom effects.
- Type: Verified principle (official guidance)
- Evidence: S3 (official WHO guidance; addressed to: the public and health workers, for a bite where transport to care is possible)
- Conditions and limits: Assumes other people can carry the victim and that transport exists. A lone person can't be carried, so the situation differs, and how "you" respond is Architecture's decision. Because *Bothrops* bites cause local swelling (F05), WHO's pressure-bandage exception would not apply. That is an inference from F05 and S3, labelled as such.
- Confidence: High
- Safety level: Open (the first-aid steps); antivenom and medical treatment are Principle only
- Story relevance: A myth correction (no cutting, no sucking, no tourniquet), and the honest bind of being alone.

F07: In a GPS study, walkers in a German forest and in the Sahara kept a straight path only when the sun or moon was visible. When clouds hid the sun, they walked in circles without noticing, and some crossed their own paths. Blindfolded, most walked in circles, sometimes under 20 m across, veering left or right with no consistent bias. The researchers attribute this to small random errors in the senses adding up, and conclude that people need external cues such as a distant landmark or the sun's position. In their words, people may be "convinced that they are walking in a straight line".
- Type: Verified principle
- Evidence: S5 (official press release of the researchers' institute, describing a peer-reviewed paper in *Current Biology*, 2009; measured in: healthy volunteers walking for several hours in a temperate German forest and in desert)
- Conditions and limits: Not measured in a rainforest. Applying it to the Amazon understory is an extrapolation, supported by F08 (the canopy blocks most light). The circles under 20 m were blindfolded, not sighted walkers.
- Confidence: High (mechanism); Medium (application to rainforest)
- Safety level: Not applicable
- Story relevance: Getting lost is something the senses do to you, not carelessness, and it corrects a belief.

F08: The canopy blocks most sunlight. At the Adolpho Ducke Reserve near Manaus, vegetation blocked about 94.85% of incoming solar radiation, and the understory was up to 4.0 °C cooler by day with about 15% higher relative humidity than a clearing (measured December 2020 to June 2021). Measurements from 1967 in Lower Amazon forests found daily mean light at ground level of roughly 1–4% of full light (terra firme, várzea and igapó forest).
- Type: Verified principle
- Evidence: S16 (peer-reviewed regional journal; abstract only; one reserve in the wet season); S15 (1967 expedition field report; primary measurements at a few sites)
- Conditions and limits: Varies with forest type, gaps and season. The two sources use different measures (blocked radiation and relative light).
- Confidence: Medium
- Safety level: Not applicable
- Story relevance: Explains why the sun often can't serve as a compass, and why it stays dim and damp.

F09: Extrapolation: under a closed canopy, the sun's position is often not usable as a direction cue, so the circling described in F07 is plausible for someone lost in the Amazon understory.
- Type: Scenario assumption (range: from the sun usable in gaps, clearings and on riverbanks, to not usable under closed canopy on overcast days; depends on: canopy gaps, cloud cover, time of day; basis: inferred from F07 and F08, not measured in a rainforest)
- Evidence: S5, S15, S16 (see F07 and F08)
- Conditions and limits: An extrapolation, not a measured finding. It may never be stated as fact or as guidance.
- Confidence: Medium-Low
- Safety level: Not applicable
- Story relevance: Lets Architecture make getting lost believable without inventing a mechanism.

F10: Official North American lost-person guidance:
  - **West Virginia** (Emergency Management Division's Search and Rescue Coordinator, via the state Division of Natural Resources): the moment you realise you're lost, stop moving; staying put is often best; panic and wandering make it harder for searchers to find you. Signal in threes (whistle blasts or flashlight flashes). If you need to move, stay in open, visible areas. Following a river or stream can often lead to civilisation, because people settle near water.
  - **AdventureSmart** (British Columbia): STOP (Sit, Think, Observe, Plan); staying put reduces the search time and area; seek shelter and stay warm and dry; signal in threes; make a ground-to-air "V" or "SOS" at least 3 m long.
- Type: Verified principle (official guidance)
- Evidence: S13, S14 (official and public-safety guidance; addressed to: hikers and hunters in West Virginia and British Columbia, where organised search and rescue exists and trip plans are expected)
- Conditions and limits: Written for temperate North America, assuming someone will search. If nobody knows "you" are missing, the logic of staying put changes. The two pieces of advice (stay put; follow water if you must move) pull in different directions; whether "you" follow either is Architecture's decision. No Amazon-specific guidance was found (X07).
- Confidence: High (as guidance); Medium (fit to the Amazon)
- Safety level: Open
- Story relevance: A genuine dilemma, to stay or to move, rooted in real guidance.

F11: Exposure of the feet to warm water for 48 hours or more causes "tropical immersion foot" (warm-water immersion foot). It was common among troops in Vietnam who waded repeatedly in paddy fields or streams.
- Type: Verified principle
- Evidence: S8 (US National Library of Medicine thesaurus scope note, citing *Andrews' Diseases of the Skin*; measured in: soldiers)
- Conditions and limits: Gives the exposure threshold only. Symptoms and progression are not in the source (X03).
- Confidence: Medium-High
- Safety level: Open (recognition)
- Story relevance: Never drying out has a clock, and it starts at about two days.

F12: NHS advice for cuts and grazes: most heal at home within a few days. Stop any bleeding; clean the wound by rinsing it with bottled or tap water; cover it; keep the dressing clean and dry. Get urgent help if: the wound has soil, pus or body fluids in it or stays dirty after cleaning; it becomes swollen, red and more painful, or pus comes out; or you feel generally unwell or have a high temperature.
- Type: Verified principle (official guidance)
- Evidence: S6 (official NHS guidance, last reviewed 2 April 2026; addressed to: the UK public, with clean water, dressings and healthcare available)
- Conditions and limits: "You" would lack clean water and dressings, and can't reach care. The guidance gives no timeline for infection.
- Confidence: High
- Safety level: Open
- Story relevance: Gives the visible stages by which a small cut becomes a worry.

F13: Cellulitis, a bacterial skin infection, makes skin painful, hot and swollen. It usually looks red, though this may be less obvious on brown or black skin, and it may blister. It is treated with antibiotics, and most people recover in 7–10 days.
- Type: Verified principle
- Evidence: S7 (official NHS guidance, last reviewed 18 April 2024; addressed to: the UK public)
- Conditions and limits: No progression timeline without treatment.
- Confidence: High
- Safety level: Open (recognition); antibiotic treatment is Principle only
- Story relevance: What "spreading" infection looks like, accurately. The note on skin tone matters for how the visuals show "you".

F14: The microbiological quality of water can't be judged by sight, smell or taste. Surface water in austere situations is likely to be poor in quality. Organisms with a low infectious dose (for example *Giardia*, *Cryptosporidium*, *Shigella* and norovirus) can cause illness even from small amounts.
- Type: Verified principle
- Evidence: S9 (Wilderness Medical Society clinical practice guideline, 2024; addressed to: wilderness travellers, international travellers and austere situations)
- Conditions and limits: Not Amazon-specific. Gives no timing for illness onset.
- Confidence: High
- Safety level: Open
- Story relevance: Clear-looking water is no proof. A temptation with a real cost.

F15: Bringing water to a boil kills pathogens (strong recommendation, high-quality evidence). Organisms that cause diarrhoea die within seconds at boiling and rapidly above 60 °C. CDC recommends boiling for 1 minute to allow for user error and add a safety margin.
- Type: Verified principle (guidance)
- Evidence: S9 (as F14)
- Conditions and limits: Needs a fire and a heat-safe container.
- Confidence: High
- Safety level: Open
- Story relevance: The safe answer depends on resources "you" may not have.

F16: Solar disinfection: clear water in a clear plastic bottle, exposed to full sunlight for 4–6 hours (or about 2 days if cloudy), significantly reduces and possibly eliminates microorganisms (strong recommendation, moderate-quality evidence).
- Type: Verified principle (guidance)
- Evidence: S9 (as F14)
- Conditions and limits: Needs full sun, clear water and a clear bottle. Under a closed canopy (F08), full sun is scarce; that is an inference combining F08 and S9. A clearing or riverbank would differ.
- Confidence: High (method); Medium (feasibility in the understory)
- Safety level: Open
- Story relevance: A real method that the forest itself makes hard.

F17: South Australia's health department says rainwater collected from a roof is generally safe to drink if it is clear, has little taste or smell, and comes from a well-maintained system. Contamination comes mainly from the catchment: dust, bird droppings, leaves and debris, especially in the first flow after a dry spell. Boiling is suggested for people with weakened immunity.
- Type: Verified principle (guidance)
- Evidence: S10 (official state health guidance, last updated 3 March 2026; addressed to: Australian households with roof tanks)
- Conditions and limits: Not about forests. That rain caught directly is cleaner than water run off leaves or bark is an extrapolation and was not verified. Treat it as a scenario assumption at most.
- Confidence: High (for roof systems); Low (for forest application)
- Safety level: Open
- Story relevance: Rain may be the safest water "you" have. This undercuts any "surrounded by water, none drinkable" framing.

F18: At the Ducke Reserve, about 46.79% of rainfall was lost to canopy interception in the understory area: caught by the canopy rather than reaching the ground directly.
- Type: Verified principle
- Evidence: S16 (abstract only; one reserve, wet season)
- Conditions and limits: One site and one season. Abstract only.
- Confidence: Medium-Low
- Safety level: Not applicable
- Story relevance: Under the canopy, rain arrives as drips and runoff more than as open rainfall. This matters for collecting it (F17).

F19: Leptospirosis is particularly common in wet tropical and subtropical regions; the bacteria survive longer in warm, humid environments. It is spread by animal urine in the environment and enters through broken skin and the mucous membranes of the eyes, mouth and nose. Entry through water-softened skin is disputed. Incubation is 2–30 days, average 7–10. Symptoms start with an abrupt flu-like illness.
- Type: Verified principle
- Evidence: S11 (official ECDC factsheet, last updated 26 June 2017)
- Conditions and limits: Not Amazon-specific. Over a few days, symptoms are possible only at the short end of incubation.
- Confidence: Medium-High
- Safety level: Open (recognition)
- Story relevance: An invisible risk carried into "you" through the cut, which may outlast the story.

F20: Malaria symptoms usually start 10–15 days after a bite by an infected mosquito. It is spread mainly by female *Anopheles* mosquitoes. Common early symptoms are fever, headache and chills.
- Type: Verified principle
- Evidence: S12 (official WHO fact sheet)
- Conditions and limits: Risk varies by area.
- Confidence: High
- Safety level: Open
- Story relevance: Within a few days, insects are a misery, not a malaria crisis. Avoids a false-timeline error.

F21: Manaus 30-year climate averages: monthly minimum temperatures of 23–24 °C and maximums of 29–31 °C all year. Rainfall is about 295–383 mm a month from December to May (March the wettest, 383 mm) and about 106–128 mm in August and September.
- Type: Verified principle
- Evidence: S17 (Brazilian meteorological service's published 30-year averages; city station)
- Conditions and limits: City data. Forest understory is cooler by day (F08). INMET's own normals were not opened.
- Confidence: Medium
- Safety level: Not applicable
- Story relevance: Season choice sets how wet "you" stay. Warm nights shape what "cold" means here.

F22: A US health system's wound-care clinician says a wound "can become infected within 24 to 48 hours but some infections develop over several days, depending on the type of wound you have and your risk factors". The same organisation says cellulitis symptoms often develop quickly, may worsen within hours, and the affected area may expand over hours or days. Signs of infection it lists include redness and firmness around the wound, thick yellow, white or green drainage, a foul smell, and more pain than expected.
- Type: Scenario assumption (range: signs of infection appearing from about 1 to 2 days after the cut, up to several days; depends on: the type of wound, contamination and personal risk factors; basis: one clinician's statement published by a health system, addressed to the general public)
- Evidence: S18, S19 (health-system patient information from one organisation, Banner Health; not independent of each other)
- Conditions and limits: One organisation only, so it does not meet the higher bar for medical claims (X01 still applies). It may be depicted as what happens to this character's cut within that range. It may never be stated in narration as a general timeline or as guidance. Not specific to heat or humidity (X02).
- Confidence: Low-Medium
- Safety level: Open (recognising signs); treatment is Principle only
- Story relevance: Makes a cut that shows clear signs of infection by day 2 or 3 a supportable scenario choice, without claiming a rule.

## PART B: STORY LEVERS

### The person in the situation
- An ordinary adult visitor would likely fear jaguars and snakes. The truth behind that belief: jaguar attacks are rare and mostly involve forest workers (F01, F02); snakes are a real but specific risk to the legs (F03, F04). The belief is a character belief; the correction is verified.
- They would likely believe they can walk in a straight line. They can't, without the sun or a landmark, and they won't notice (F07, F08). This is a motivated, sympathetic mistake.
- Public first aid assumes clean water, dressings and help (F06, F12). "You" know roughly what to do but can't do it properly. Knowledge that only shifts the odds.

### Constraints and dependencies
- Safe water depends on fire and a container (F15), or on full sun (F16), which the canopy withholds (F08). Rain may be the best option (F17), but under the canopy it arrives as drips and runoff (F18).
- Direction depends on the sun or landmarks (F07), which the canopy hides (F08).
- Staying put depends on someone searching (F10). Moving depends on direction (F07).

### How things fail, and how fast
- Wet feet: tropical immersion foot needs about 48 hours or more of exposure (F11). A real clock for a multi-day story.
- A cut: the visible warning stages are verified (F12, F13); the speed is not (X01). Show the stages, not a timetable.
- Infections carried in may not show until after the story ends: leptospirosis 2–30 days (F19), malaria 10–15 days (F20). This could be a debrief or an ending's honest shadow.

### Overlooked dangers
- Water that looks clean (F14).
- Never drying out (F11, F08, F21).
- Your own sense of direction (F07).
- A small break in the skin as a doorway (F12, F19).

### Misconceptions and what they cost
- "Big cats are the main danger" → out of proportion (F01, F02). Fear itself could cost "you"; that is Architecture's choice.
- "I'm walking straight" → circles (F07).
- "Clear water is safe" → not judgeable by sight (F14).
- Snakebite folk remedies such as cutting, suction and tourniquets → WHO says avoid (F06). A myth shown must be corrected (Bible §8.3).

### Real trade-offs the situation offers
- Stay put (search logic, F10) versus move (following water, F10), when nobody may know "you" are missing.
- Drink now and risk illness (F14) versus wait for rain or a way to boil (F15, F17).
- Walk on wet, failing feet (F11) versus rest and lose daylight or progress.

### Human limits and human behaviour
- Panic and wandering make searchers' work harder (F10, guidance). The research on stress and decision-making was not opened (X10), so how stress affects judgement stays general.

### Physical and environmental texture
- Dim understory, roughly 1–5% of full light at ground level (F08); cooler and more humid than clearings (F08).
- Rain as drips through the canopy (F18); heavy months from December to May (F21).
- Warm nights, 23–24 °C minimums (F21).

### What the story could teach, and how knowledge could figure in it
- Knowledge that shifts the odds: knowing water can't be judged by sight (F14); knowing to stop rather than wander (F10).
- Knowledge that arrives too late or explains afterwards: walking in circles (F07); immersion foot (F11).
- Knowledge that corrects a myth through consequence: snakebite folk remedies (F06).
- Knowledge whose results come after the story: incubation periods (F19, F20).

### Strongest material
- **Walking in circles (F07 + F08).** A surprising, well-sourced mechanism that makes "lost close to camp" credible in feeling, and corrects a belief viewers hold about themselves.
- **The 48-hour wet-feet clock (F11).** An ordinary, overlooked danger with a real threshold that fits a multi-day story.
- **Water you can't read (F14–F17).** A trade-off chain (fire, sun, rain) where the forest blocks each easy answer.
- **Honest animal scale (F01–F06).** Jaguars are rare; snakes are real and specific. Fear can be bigger than the risk, while one risk stays real.
- **Dangers that outlast the story (F19, F20).** A grounded, unshowy way to end on consequence.
