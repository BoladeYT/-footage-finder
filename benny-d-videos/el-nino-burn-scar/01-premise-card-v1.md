## PREMISE CARD: El Niño burn scar
Status: Draft, awaiting your go

**Working promise:** You've just moved into a California home below a hillside that burned in last year's wildfire, and the first big storm of an El Niño winter is arriving.

**The premise:** "You" are an ordinary person who recently moved into a quiet California neighborhood, below a slope burned in a recent wildfire, and who doesn't really know what that burned hill means. It's an El Niño winter, which may make heavy winter rain more likely in California (to check). A storm arrives, a warning comes, and at first it may look like ordinary rain. "You" have to decide what to believe and what to do before the danger is obvious.

**Why this version:** A hazard most viewers have never heard of, in an ordinary American home, seen through a newcomer who discovers it at the same moment the viewer does, with El Niño explained honestly as the reason the odds were higher, not as the cause of one storm.

**What's worth understanding (unverified):** Why burned slopes can turn heavy rain into fast debris flows; why a short burst of intense rain may matter more than the day's total (to check); what the different warnings and evacuation levels mean and why people underestimate them; and what El Niño does and doesn't do to a US winter: it shifts the odds for a season rather than causing a single storm (to check).

**Likely engine (unverified):** Information and deadline: deciding what to believe and when to act, while a window to act safely may be closing.

**Questions for Research (to check)**
1. What is the current ENSO status and outlook for winter 2026–27 (NOAA Climate Prediction Center): is an El Niño under way or expected, and how strong? This decides how the story can describe "this winter".
2. What does the evidence say about El Niño and California winter rain, especially Southern California: how strong and how reliable is the link, and have strong El Niño winters delivered less than expected (for example 2015–16)?
3. How do post-fire debris flows work: why burned slopes produce them, how long after a fire the risk lasts, and what rainfall intensity triggers them (USGS thresholds, as a range)? How fast do they move, and how much warning do people realistically get?
4. Which warnings and alerts would a California resident actually receive (National Weather Service flash flood watches and warnings for burn areas, county evacuation warnings versus orders, Wireless Emergency Alerts), and what does each one mean?
5. What does official public guidance say to do before and during a debris-flow threat, including if "you" can't leave in time? Which parts are Open and which are principle only?
6. How do residents typically respond to evacuation warnings in burn areas (for example warning fatigue or underestimating rain), and would a newcomer plausibly not know about the burn-scar risk? Any claim about how people behave needs a source.
